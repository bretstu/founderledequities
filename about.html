How every number is made.

What every CEO owns of the company they run, computed from the filings they signed. This page is the definition, the sources, and the rules, in plain language.

Why founders

When you buy a stock you own a piece of a company but almost none of the say. The direction belongs to whoever runs it, so the question behind every stock is a personnel question: who is deciding, and what happens to them if they're wrong?

Most CEOs are hired, and paid mostly in options: upside without downside, on a timer. A founder who still holds a large stake is different. Their stake does not expire. If the company loses, they lose, in the largest position of their life, for as long as they hold it. About one in ten large public companies is still run by a person who started it. This site keeps score: who owns what, whether it's growing or shrinking, and what they did with their own money.

How ownership is calculated

ownership = shares the CEO holds ÷ shares the company has outstanding The same denominator for every CEO, so every figure on the site is comparable.

Counted

  • Issued shares held directly or through trusts, holding companies and family vehicles that are theirs.
  • Every class of common stock; a convertible class counts at its conversion ratio.
  • Unvested restricted stock: it is issued, it votes, and it is in the share count. The row says when a stake includes it.

Not counted

  • Options and restricted stock units. They are promises of shares, not shares: not issued, not in the count.
  • Shares a filing attributes to the CEO that the company itself says are not theirs. Rare, and every such case is listed with its source.
  • Partnership units and other economics held outside common stock. Where that is the whole stake (Blackstone), the site prints no number rather than a misleading one.

Where the numbers come from

Shares held come from the tables of SEC Forms 3, 4 and 5, the filings the CEO signs within two business days of a trade. For each share class the site takes the newest filing that reports it and sums its lines, so a stake is what the CEO's own latest report says it is. Shares outstanding come from the cover page of the company's newest 10-Q or 10-K, carried through stock splits. Nothing is estimated.

Why this differs from other numbers you'll see

The SEC's beneficial ownership figure, the one in a proxy statement or a Schedule 13G, is a legal test, not a measure of what someone owns. It adds shares the person could acquire within 60 days (vested options, RSUs about to settle) to both the top and the bottom of the fraction, and it includes shares attributed by rule even when the person disclaims them. That is why a headline can say 48% where this site says 46%. Voting power is a third thing again: shares weighted by the votes their class carries. At a company with super-voting stock it can be double the economic stake. This site measures economic ownership: the shares whose value rises and falls with the stock today.

The one exception, and how it's handled

Occasionally a filer states in a remark that shares they hold of record are not in their filing's tables. The site reads tables, never prose, so no software turns that sentence into a number. A person does, once, and records it with the filing as the source. The row then shows both figures, the total and the part the tables report, and the entry retires itself the day a filing reports those shares in a table or stops stating the figure.

What counts as a trade

Bought and sold mean the stake moved: shares acquired on the open market, or shares sold from the position. Planned marks a sale made under a 10b5-1 plan set months or years earlier; discretionary means the person chose the day. The distinction matters: most CEO selling is planned, and the rare open-market purchase is the signal.

Compensation is what the company gave and what was sold of it: awards granted, options exercised and held or sold the same day, vests with tax withheld, units converted and sold. Those trades did not move the stake the way a purchase or a sale does, and they are listed, named, and left out of every ranking, so a "$50M sale" that was options cashed is never counted as a founder reducing their stake. Gifts, conversions and catch-up filings for trades made before the company was public are shown as transfers, on the same terms.

Two dates appear on every trade: the day it happened and the day it reached EDGAR. Filers have two business days, and catch-up filings after an IPO can carry months of trades at once.

Who is a founder

The company's own proxy statement decides. If it names the CEO a founder or co-founder, the row carries the badge and the sentence that earned it. An heir who inherited the chair, or a founder who moved to the board while someone else runs the company, is not a founder-CEO. Where the proxy's language is ambiguous the badge reads "founder?" rather than guessing. The classification is reviewed by hand against the largest stakes and corrected at the source when it is wrong.

Founders against the index

An equal-weight basket of the founder-led companies, rebalanced monthly from split-adjusted month-end closes with dividends excluded on every line, against the S&P 500 (SPY) and, because equal weight against cap weight mixes two effects, the equal-weight S&P 500 (RSP). Companies enter when their price history begins. You can view the S&P 500's founders or every founder-led company worth $1B or more.

A portrait, not a strategy. The basket is today's founder-led companies applied backward. Founders who left, companies acquired or delisted, and companies that fell below $1B are not in it, and they are disproportionately the losers. That flatters the line, and the distribution beneath the chart shows how much of the result came from a few large winners.

The confidence marks

high the newest filing reconciles with the record and the share count is fresh. medium a judgment was made along the way (a convertible class, a restated balance) and the row says which. low something known is missing or the figures don't reconcile; treat the number as a lead, not a fact, and read the filing linked on the row.

How fresh it is

Everything refreshes nightly from EDGAR. Prices are the latest close. A trade can take two business days to appear in a filing, so "yesterday" on this site means the filings available last night.

The S&P 500 is free. The other ~1,600 companies worth $1B or more are the Pro tier, $5 a month, cancel any time. Pro is more companies, not a different lens: nothing is computed differently behind the seal.

Who makes this

One person, nights and weekends. No firm behind it, no positions to promote, nothing for sale beyond the site. Credibility can't rest on a brand name, so it rests on method and receipts: every figure links to the filing it came from, and when the data can't be reconciled the site says so.

A number that disagrees with a filing is the most useful message I can get: corrections@founderledequities.com. Ideas and requests: hello@founderledequities.com.

The fine print

Nothing here is investment advice. The site reports what filings say; it recommends nothing, and a large founder stake is not by itself a reason to own a stock. Accuracy is pursued, not guaranteed: filings contain errors, filers are inconsistent, software has bugs. Where this site and a filing disagree, the filing is right. Visits are counted by the site itself, without cookies: which sections were read and which buttons pressed, no email, no IP address, nothing shared.
