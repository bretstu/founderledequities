Why founders.

What chief executives own of the companies they run: why it's worth measuring, who makes this, and exactly how every number here is made.

The idea

When you buy a stock you become an owner of a company. Owners get almost no say in the vision. You can vote on a few things, and you can sell. The direction belongs to whoever is running the place, which means the real question behind every stock is a personnel question: who is making the decisions, and what happens to them if the decisions go wrong?

For most large companies the person steering is a hired executive, paid mostly in options and grants. Hired doesn't mean bad. Old companies can't be founder-led, and plenty of hired chief executives are excellent. But an option has a particular shape: upside without downside, on a timer. It costs its holder nothing if the stock falls, pays if the stock is up before it expires, and is settled by issuing new shares, diluting everyone who actually owns the company. Ideas that pay off in year ten are weighed differently when pay is decided by the stock price next year.

A founder who still holds a large stake is a different animal. The vision is theirs, not adopted, and their stake does not expire. If the company loses, they lose, usually in the largest financial position of their life, for as long as they hold it. Their money sits on the same side of the table as yours.

Roughly one in ten large public companies is still run by a person who started it. This site keeps score: who owns what, whether the stake is growing or shrinking, and what each executive actually did with their own money: bought, sold, or merely cashed the compensation.

Who makes this

One person. The information behind this site has always been public. Every officer of a public company reports their holdings and trades to the SEC within days, on documents they sign. But public is not the same as visible. The record sits scattered across thousands of filings written for compliance, not for reading, and the tools I tried either drowned the answer in a feed of every insider at every company or compressed it into headlines that didn't survive contact with the documents. Eventually I ran out of excuses and built the thing I wanted: nights and weekends, reading filings, turning them into something a human can look at.

Independent, no firm behind it, no positions to promote, nothing for sale beyond the site itself. That means credibility can't rest on a brand name, so it rests on the only thing better: method and receipts. The counting rules are written below, every number links to its source, and when the data can't be reconciled the site says so instead of papering over it. If a number looks wrong, tell me, and I will go read the filing with you: corrections@founderledequities.com.

How the numbers are made

Every figure is computed from structured SEC filings. Nothing is estimated, scraped from an aggregator, or taken from a news story.

The shares held come from the ownership forms each executive signs, Forms 3, 4 and 5, read in full, every filing since the record begins, with amendments displacing what they restate. Balances are carried per vehicle (a trust reported once keeps its balance until the filer mentions it again), which is how the forms themselves work.

The denominator is the company's own share count, taken from the cover page of its quarterly and annual reports (the 10-Q and the 10-K), carried through stock splits so a split never reads as a change in ownership.

Unexercised options are never counted as ownership. An option is a claim on shares that do not exist yet. They are tracked and reported separately, and when an executive exercises and sells the same day the feed labels it options cashed instead of calling compensation a change of heart.

A sale that does not move the stake is recorded, linked, and kept out of every summary. Options cashed, and units or convertibles exchanged into shares and sold the same day, are real sales of real shares; the common-stock position this site measures ends where it began. They appear in the full trade table, badged for what they are, and nowhere else: not in the day's list, the buy and sell rankings, the totals, or the three-year changes. The known limit is the holder whose economics sit outside common stock — Blackstone's chief executive holds partnership units, so his sales of exchanged units are real reductions of his stake that this measurement cannot see; the site says so on his row rather than printing a number it cannot stand behind.

Partial filings are detected by arithmetic, and shown honestly. Form 4's own rules let a filer report only the vehicles a transaction touched; the trusts can stay lawfully silent. Followed literally, a filing like that makes a stake look like it cratered when nothing was sold. The site checks every step of every record for balance changes no transaction explains. When a later filing puts the balance back, the rows in between are marked partial, excluded from every summary (charts, peaks, troughs, the lenses) and kept in the full record with a badge saying exactly what they are. When the newest filing looks partial, nothing can settle it until the next one arrives, so the figure is shown as filed, with a caution, and the company's confidence mark drops until the record reconciles.

Prices are as filed. Trade values use the execution prices each Form 4 reports. The rare filer error, a price a thousand times off the market, is flagged and shown as filed, never silently corrected, because a number you can trace to its document beats a number someone quietly fixed.

The performance chart is a portrait, not a strategy, and the methodology has a known bias. The founders line is an equal-weight index of the companies the record calls founder-led today, rebalanced monthly from adjusted month-end closes. Companies enter when their price history begins, and the benchmark is the S&P 500 via the SPY ETF. Applying today's cohort backward is survivorship bias: companies whose founders left, or that dropped out of the index, are not represented, and that flatters the founders line. The chart carries this warning where it is drawn. Read the line as a description of how the current cohort got here, not as a strategy anyone could have followed.

Which companies are covered is decided by written rules, and the list is published. The site covers every US company that files a 10-K, has ownership filings on record, and had a market capitalization at or above $1 billion on a dated quarterly snapshot; foreign filers are absent because their officers file no ownership forms, not by choice. Two independent market-cap measures decide size, disagreements are flagged for a human, and a company leaves only after two snapshots below $800 million so the edge does not flap. Every member, the snapshot date, and the full rules are on the universe page. Until that page exists here, the site covers the S&P 500.

And the standing invitation: every trade on the site links to the filing it came from. You never have to take a number on trust. Click through and read the document yourself.

The fine print

Nothing here is investment advice. This site reports what securities filings say. It does not recommend buying, selling, or holding anything, and a large founder stake is not, by itself, a reason to own a stock. Value is subjective: two people can look at the same founder holding the same stake and reach opposite conclusions.

Accuracy is pursued, not guaranteed. Filings contain errors, filers are inconsistent, and software has bugs. The confidence marks exist precisely because certainty isn't uniform. Where this site and a filing disagree, the filing is right; SEC EDGAR is the source of truth, and every figure here links back to it.

Visits are counted by the site itself, without cookies. To learn whether anyone comes here and what they look at, the page notes which sections were read and which buttons were pressed and sends that to its own database. No cookie is set, no email or IP address is kept, and the only identifier is a random one your browser holds for itself. Nothing is shared with anyone.

Figures can lag. Data refreshes from EDGAR on a nightly cycle; insiders have up to two business days to report a trade. What you see is as current as the filings allow.
